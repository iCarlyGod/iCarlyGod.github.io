## Why is making good games important?

I don't think it needs to explained why people want and like good games.  People want to take a break from reality and experience a more exciting world.  People also look for certain experiences that they can't experience themselves in their real lives.  That is obviously very understandable and it's why I've enjoyed the games I have.  This section is more for the out-of-touch people that oversee the production of games, but this could also applies to other forms of fictional media at some points too.

Making good games is important from a business perspective.  Usually, it means rights to an IP that its audience trusts.  Tears of the Kingdom would not be as highly anticipated or talked about if Breath of the Wild was not talked about with such high regards all around.  This is not only true just for the IP as positive reception usually leads to other games under the same studio being sold.  To speak of example from my own experience, I only played Ghost Trick: Phanton Detective because it was written by the guy who wrote Ace Attorney, and I'm pretty sure I'm with the majority when it comes to the reason I played the game.  In short, making a good game means the consumers trust the quality of all products a studio pushes out, and it's also why people trust first-party merchandise too.

## So what do players want that makes a game good?

In my experience, there are three broader video game audiences: the audience that wants a narrative to follow, the audience that wants to win over other people, and the audience that wants to be something they aren't in real life.  Sometimes, games can overlap in audiences or fit to a particular subgroup of one of the audiences, but I think these are the three sort of high-level reasons people want to buy and play video games.

The competitive audience is better known as the "Esports community" and try to legitimize video games as a form of sport.  Its legitimacy can be a discussion for another post, but regardless, there is still an audience for those sort of games.  These games tend to be either imitations of board games, or type of competitive games that are not possible in the real world.  No one can throw fireballs and do jumping uppercuts like in Street Fighter, or be both a kid and squid like in Splatoon, but that's their charm; they're unrealistic and create a unique environment for strategy and skills to master.

The narrative audience wants a game that plays like the enhancement of a movie, novel, or comic.  I think the clearest example I can give is a mystery novel versus a mystery visual novel, where games will allow the player to solve the mystery themselves and complete puzzles to progress in the story.  This allows the player to experience deeper emersion in the narrative and feel more like they are the protagonist themselves sometimes.  I would say this has the smallest audience of the three groups simply because people don't have the attention span for more narrative-oriented games, but they still find their points of success and charm either way.

Originally, I wanted to lump this with the narrative audience, but I thought the roleplaying audience wanted a more general sense of accomplishment.  Roleplaying games have a narrative, but roleplays are more about the player's interaction with the fictional environment created than the quality of the narrative of the roleplay itself.  It's usually a means to be something you aren't and accomplish something you wouldn't accomplish in the real world.  How else would you become the champion of pitting mythical creatures that shoot fire and cause earthquakes against each other if not for Pokemon?  How else would you live of a paradise island with a bunch of large-headed animal-humanoid strangers than Animal Crossing?  Unlike the narrative games, these are more about the adventures and the journey rather than the masterfully told story.  These games try and make the player feel as if they accomplished something grand or impressive as their main point of appeal.

Sometimes, these games overlap in their audience they try to capture.  Pokemon, which originally was only ever viewed as roleplaying game, has become pretty competitive games in both single and double battles.  Final Fantasy VII is a JRPG with a legacy of having one of the greatest narratives of all video games.  Super Smash Bros. Brawl had a brilliant RPG story mode on top of being a party fighting game.  There are more examples than this, but they were the first ones to pop in my head.  Game devs generally try to follow the idea that a game should be fun, or present new ideas and experiences, and they usually achieve it by pandering to at least one of the ideas I've given.  Video games are brilliant for the variety of experiences they can bring, and you can really tell how much care and effort goes into a game.

## Closing thoughts

I really like video games.  I really like all sorts of video games, and I think I've delved into enough to generally understand them at a fundamental level. I might talk about that in a later post, but the point stands that games try too often to capture a younger market with video games and try to take easy pickings by using established IPs for commercial success.  Games should just be made to be games, and I don't see why nor do I like how everything is trying to be a tool for profit in the modern age.
