Ever since I first learned about the world, which is almost a decade now, I still see the word "metagame" be used or perceived incorrectly and it is never improving.  So, I thought I'd made a post talking about it before I get more migraines about it because I think people misconceive the idea of a metagame and why it exists in all competitive games.  I have been watching competitive games for over a decade and competed at a high level too, so I think I have enough of a background to talk about a pretty basic topic in the competitive landscape.

## What is NOT the metagame?

People usually misconceive the meta as the
* Most
* Effective
* Tactic(s)
* Available
which, for a super-quick super-comprehensible explanation, works... kind of. The problem with this definition is that it lacks the nuance in the idea that metagames are not objective and can depend with time periods, skill levels, and balance updates.

People also often cite the metagame as the "the game outside of the game" which I also think is lacking in key ideas of the metagame at least in the context it is most misused in: video games.  

It's pretty funny how these misconceptions of the metagame lacks the nuance of the other explanation, but that's only because meta is a concept that cannot be simply explained with just a single sentence.  The idea is too abstract for people to comprehend in a single sentence.

## So what IS the metagame?

The metagame is a still snapshot of the relative strengths of adjacent options in a given everchanging competitive environment.  That would be how I would explain it in a single sentence, but I find most people would find that hard to understand.  Let me break it down for you.

* A "still snapshot" refers to a single state of a game that constantly evolves.
* "Relative strengths" refers to the idea that certain choices will have more favourable outcomes than others.
* "Adjacent options" refers to a set of choices a player can make at a given point in a game.  This usually is discussed in the form of character selections.
* "Given competitive environment" refers to the medium of which the competition occurs in.  For the sake of my post, this usually means the competitive video game itself, but it can be broken down further into the skill level of the players in a given match, the version of the game being played, the characters the players select, etc.  This basically includes anything about the game where variance occurs.

Still too dry?  Let me give you an example.  Let's take rock-paper-scissors, the simplest form of a competitive game.  Let's say the options get picked at the following frequencies
* Rock gets picked 60% of the time, because it takes the least effort to use and people like hammering their opponent's fingers.  It's also humanly impossible to react to.
* Paper gets picked 30% of the time, because it beats the most frequent option, rock, but it takes the most effort to do and some people can react to the hand opening.
* Scissors gets picked 10% of the time, because no one want to lose to rock.  It only gets picked when people see their opponents opening their hand for paper, which then they quickly switch to scissors.

Will this be true forever?  No.  This is a **still snapshot** of the weight probabilities people use rock, paper, and scissors in this **given competitive environemnt**.  People understand that rock is, **relatively, the strongest** option, because it is the hardest to react to of the **given options**.  Eventually, more people will try to use paper to beat rock, and then use scissors to beat the uprise in paper usage.  The idea of a metagame is to express what are the strong and weak choices in whatever context understanding the relative strength between choices is currently important in.  

## Roles in a metagame

A metagame is usually seen as a complex web of relationships.  The role of an option in a metagame is determine by the given option's relationships with the most relevant options in a metagame.  Here are some of the most generally refered to roles of a metagame:

### Best option in the metagame

This is self explanatory.  Usually this is the option the metagame revolves around either due to is oppresive strengths, its ease of use, and/or its flexibility.  The options that fit this role are usually disliked by more casual players for their high pick rates, but they are important as they allow for deeper strategy.  Using the RPS example, because rock gets picked at such high frequency in the given situation, it forces players to figure out how to beat people that pick rock very often and it forces those that pick rock very often to figure out how to beat the people that try to beat rock.  A clear best option in a metagame usually allows for more definitive layers in strategy than a metagame with a lack of one.  Of course, there are degenerate best options that may hurt the competitive nature of a game, but they are rare as they usually have extremely oppressive strengths and low flexibility leading to one-dimensional play. 

### Niche picks

These options are overall worse than the ones at the pinnacle of the metagame, but they have a specific set of strengths and weaknesses that allows them to pick apart certain parts of the metagame.  They are fringe, extremely situational picks that usually have a few notable performances before garnering too much attention to see much success after that.  They sort of rely on some surprise factor in order to have an impact in whatever game it's being used in, which is partially why they are rare in the first place.  Using the RPS example, the most niche pick would be scissors for it's extremely low pick rate and it's strength in situations where a player thinks their opponent will not pick rock.  However, if too many people pick scissors, they will start being increasing aware of the possibility that their opponents picks scissors and defaults to rock.  

### Anti-meta

This is sort of the middleground between being "meta" and being "niche" as they try to approach the game from a completely different direction than those that choose to follow the meta.  This is more often seen in games with compositions like with card game decks and team lineups.  These differ from niche in that the entire strategy is niche instead of a infrequent, fringe pick.  Using the RPS example, an anti-meta choice would to pick paper at high frequency to beat the rock-choosers while also not losing to paper-choosers.  The high paper pick rate is used to directly counter the high rock pick rate.  It's possible for the meta to be overtaken by anti-meta if enough people make the shift, but usually the meta shifts to beat the anti-meta in games more complicated than RPS.

### Off meta

Off meta is slightly different from anti-meta in that although it doesn't follow the standard of the meta, but instead it tries to innovate the meta to meet its own standards for comfort.  Sometimes, people feel comfortable picking certain options and making the adaptations from those comfort options as opposed to following the flow of adaptations of the standard meta.  This is most commonly seen with team games as adapting team coordination is more difficult than just adapting as a single individual.

### Outclassed options

This is the type of options I think people do not understand the most when it comes to a metagame.  These options have poorer matchup spreads across the board, but, unlike the niche picks, their set of strengths and weaknesses do not fit in the metagame as another option does what it does better.  If we use Pokemon as an example, there would be no reason to use Squirtle over Blastoise in the context of trying to win a Pokemon battle.  Blastoise has better stats, more moves, and everything Squirtle has, so the only purpose of using Squirtle would be to flex on your opponent I guess.

## Misconceptions of a metagame

I've talked about a few already in the previous sections, but it really bugs me that there is a lack of a change in the perception of a metagame in the last decade or so.  There is a large disconnect in the minds of the players and the spectators in all competitive games and sports, but I'll probably talk about that in a later post if I feel like it.  My point is that people don't seem to care to understand competition anymore and just want to feel part of a big united group, and because there is such a lack of understanding of the inner workings of the game/sport amongst the majority that indulge in the competition, acts praise and hatred are not properly given where due.  So I want to do my part and explain certain misconceptions.

### "Using top picks in a metagame is lame and lacks creativity"

This is probably the most common perception amongst the uninformed/unintelligent, and it is simply not true.  As the primary threat(s) in the metagame, they tend to have the most flexibility and adaptability of the options in the metagame.  The ability to fill in a variety of roles and still be a threat is a threat in of itself as it adds to the number of possibilities the opponent has to cover.  The best example of this is Fox in pretty much any Super Smash Bros. game, but especially in Melee.  

In Melee, Fox has some of the fastest movement in the game, the fastest move in the game, high-damaging combos, and strong kill moves and setups.  It's the reason he is a contender for the best character in the game, but if you are a Melee fan like me, you also know Fox is very versatile.  Fox can use his movement speed and his attack speed to rush down opponents and pressure opponents with a lot of attacks that lead to high-damaging combos or even KOs.  However, Fox can also be incredibly defensive by taking advantage of his speed to get away from his opponents and force his opponents to commit to an approach.  Fox players have the freedom to choose how defensive or aggressive they can play, but they also can choose how they play defensive and aggressive too.  When playing Fox agressively, the player can take advantage of Fox's ground speed to quickly close horizontal space, or they can take advantage of Fox's fast fall speed to swiftly drop down onto his opponents with quick aerial attacks.  Defensively, Fox players can take advantage of Fox's deceptively large hitboxes to catch opponents on their approach, or they can abuse Fox's swiftness to whiff punish (attacking an opponent after they miss an attack) the opponent's approach.  I haven't even mentioned the freedom Fox has when he combos his opponents.  The amount of creativity you can have with Fox combos is uncountable and you kind of have to see it to believe it.  The variety of options against every character and the ability to play in so many different ways is why I personally find Fox to be the best character in Melee.  I know I said a lot of words, but I think you get the idea that standard meta picks can have the ability to play in a number of different ways because they already have the best tools in the game.

### "Trying to win is cringe; people should pick their favourites to win"

This is the most overused, incorrect perception of a competitive game I know of.  This comes from Pokemon and it is primarily used in the context of competitive Pokemon.  Here is the full quote:

![This isn't even the full quote](https://encrypted-tbn0.gstatic.com/images?q=tbn:ANd9GcTDeA77sKAnkL9av2tZvsC-wnaNrtrdwCtZFXE7IBYdaw&s) 

I personally have no clue to the exact reason(s) why people echo this philosophy, but using the context of Pokemon, how much you love your Pokemon will not increase your chances at winning.  There are reasons that make the strong Pokemon strong and the weak ones weak.  When there are a finite set of options for a player, there will be an option that works better than the rest and every little advantage counts in improving one's chances at winning.  

It is then people often point out [a small electric squirrel name Pachirisu winning the world championships.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M7Ki5Fo1FKQ)  As impressive as it was to win the world championships that year with a Pokemon no one would have conceived had a chance in the first place, it was not amongst the world champion's favourite Pokemon despite Pachirisu becoming this player's entire online identity after winning this tournament.  The choice was calculated and it filled a niche on an anti-meta team.  It's also important to note that the format of Pokemon battles in the Pokemon World Championships that year only allowed the ~450/600 Pokemon obtainable through normal play in Pokemon X and Y (so no transfering from other games or event Pokemon) and it was the first world championships that even had the game-warping mega evolution in play.  This meant the meta was young, frail, volatile, and constraining on what Pokemon a player could use, so it was an opportune time for a Pokemon on nobody's radars, like Pachirisu, to get a win like this.  This is not to discredit the player, of course, but it's to acknowledge the genius of the choice and to not excuse his championship to just happening to blindly loving a small electric squirrel that also happened to be super useful in allowing him to him the world championships.  

Logical reasoning usually wins out over blind love in any sort of competition.  This is not to say logical reasoning should be the sole factor in one's selections as comfort and confidence are also extremely important when picking your options.  

### "I can do well with _________, so everyone is wrong when they say it's bad"

This one is more of a pet peeve of mine than anything from when I played competitive Splatoon.  I don't think this is as common of a philosphy in all parts of the world, but it was particularly frequent in the western community.  This one can really be summed up with the idea that you need a certain number of data points to make a confident conclusion, but the Splatoon community is on the younger side so I would say 90% are not old enough either to understand this concept or to have learned this in some statistics course.  There is also the fact that multiple metagames can exist for a single competitive environement simulataneously.

I have yet to talk about is the idea of several different metagames occuring in the same environment.  It isn't as simple as "there is one metagame per set of rules and game," as metagames depend on the strength of the options and ALSO the frequency at which options are picked.  The strength of the options in a metagame kind of depend on the field's abilities to use the options in the first place.  You can't play a fast character in most games if you don't have good reactions and technical ability and snipers are only scary if the sniper player has good aim.  This means at lower skill levels, weapons that have lower skill floors are better because the weapons with high skill floors cannot have their full potential reached.  This also means it is uncomfortable for less-skilled players to use high skill floor options, and then lower skill levels gravitate towards low skill floor weapons.  This means the strength of options differ depending on the skill level context and it also means the pick rate of options change.

People don't understand when community members with respect and reputation say certain options are bad, they're not speaking from a place of truth at all levels and they usually only mean in the context of the top level.  You can try to bring top level options to lower skill levels and find variable success just trying to emulate it, but whenever people do this, the lack the deeper understanding of the game to utilize the choices of the best players in the world to their maximum potential.

### "I have to use the meta to win"

This on its own is flawed, but I think the more interesting misconception is the idea you either choose between having to use the meta or try to go full off meta.  I think it's obviously very silly as there is a spectrum between standard meta and completely off meta.  When I played competitive Splatoon, it was common to see teams have 3 standard meta players and one off meta player where the three meta players would try to comfortably set up the off meta player as much as possible.  There were some cases where they just went all standard meta picks and there were some teams that had 2-3 off meta players too.  Most people, especially at low level, don't understand that the meta only really matters at the highest levels of competition and it matters less and less as you go down in skill level.

The meta has a purpose and it is to identify the primary threats in the field and what players need to focus on beating at their skill level.  It's merely a suggestion when it comes to making your own choices and it's never something you should need to follow.  This is why the metagame is "the game inside the game," because it's the game of choices that give psychological, tactical, or mechanical advantages to one side of the actual competitive game.  Standadard meta selections allow for more emotional comfort and confidence in the match, surprise factors are tactical advantages, and experience and comfort choices provide mechanical advantages in reduced mental strain.  There are probably more effects the metagame has on the actual game, but those are the ones I can think of off the top of my head.

### Standard meta picks should be nerfed so all options are equal in strength

I completely understand where this sort of philosphy comes from as there was a time where I had the same sort of belief myself.  The idea is that if you give players a certain set of options to pick from, there's no reason to not pick the options that are strictly better than the rest when the objective is to beat the opponent that has the same options to choose from.  This is especially pushed by the general gaming community because game devs have given these options personalities and human-like characteristics, and people start demanding for game balance in the name of representation.  

However, there is a harsh truth to the matter: balance is a sense of subjective perception.  As much games try to balance things, sometimes, games try to balance things too much in one direction because people don't give an option a chance until it becomes one of the best options in the game.  This can be refered to as "Power creep" as the power level of certain options "creep up" on the playerbase, and it's a simple fact that people will not make a change until they see the value of making the change exceeds the investment to making the change.  The best players in the world will only make a change if they test it and find that the change proved to improved their chances at winning without innovating or refining the play around the change.  Thus, I find it more reasonable and more enjoyable when a meta is defined and stagnant as it allows for deeper and more nuanced play and creativity.

## In Conclusion

The concept of a metagame is hard to wrap your head around.  Anything with the prefix "meta-" is hard to comprehend as the ideas with that prefix refer to some idea with multiple layers of understanding, so it's not really that surprising that a spectacle enjoyed mostly by youth fail to understand the idea of a metagame.  I think metas are the sign of a genuinely competitive game.  People aren't running around like headless chickens and a metagame shows a level of refinement in strategy.  I understand why people think badly of games that have a defined meta, but the existence of a meta is inevitable and the meta is what allows games to be deeper and more strategic.  For those that actually play games, having a metagame is a sign of competitive stability and games should be more accepting of games that have a stabilized metagame.
